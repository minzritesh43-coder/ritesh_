## 2. Light and reflection: the foundation


### 2.1 A few basic ideas

- **Light** is a form of energy. We see an object when light from that object reaches our eyes.
- In one uniform medium, light travels in a straight line. This is called **rectilinear propagation**.
- A **ray** is a line with an arrow that shows the direction of light. A **beam** is a group of rays.
- A **transparent** material lets most light pass through; a **translucent** material lets some light through; an **opaque** material does not let light pass through.
- When light falls on a surface and returns to the same medium, the process is called **reflection**.


### 2.2 Reflection and its two laws

Look at a ray falling on a plane mirror. The ray that comes in is the **incident ray**. The ray that goes back is the **reflected ray**. The point where the ray strikes is the **point of incidence**. A line drawn perpendicular to the mirror at that point is the **normal**.

1. The **incident ray, reflected ray and normal** at the point of incidence all lie in the **same plane**.
2. The **angle of incidence (i)** is always equal to the **angle of reflection (r)**: `i = r`.

> **Key point: Very important** — Both angles are measured from the **normal**, not from the mirror surface. If the ray falls along the normal, `i = 0°` and `r = 0°`, so it returns on the same path.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 2.1 — Reflection at a plane surface. Angles i and r are measured from the dashed normal.


### 2.3 Regular and diffused reflection

*Table 2.1 — Both kinds obey the same laws of reflection*

| Regular reflection | Diffused (irregular) reflection |
| --- | --- |
| Takes place on a smooth, polished surface. | Takes place on a rough or uneven surface. |
| Parallel rays remain parallel after reflection. | Parallel rays go in different directions after reflection. |
| A clear image can be formed. | No clear image is formed, but the surface becomes visible. |
| Example: plane mirror, still water. | Example: paper, wall, cloth, rough road. |

**Easy meaning:** rough paper has many tiny surfaces with different normals. Each ray follows `i = r` at its own point, so the full beam spreads out. The law is not broken.


### 2.4 Real image, virtual image and magnification

*Table 2.2 — Words used in every ray diagram*

| Term | Simple meaning | Quick test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real image | Light rays actually meet after reflection/refraction. | Can be obtained on a screen. |
| Virtual image | Rays only appear to meet when produced backwards. | Cannot be obtained on a screen. |
| Erect image | Upright, in the same direction as the object. | The arrow points upward. |
| Inverted image | Upside down compared with the object. | The arrow points downward. |
| Magnification (m) | How many times the image height is compared with object height. | `m = image height / object height`. |

For a **plane mirror**, the image is virtual, erect, the same size as the object, laterally inverted, and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. Its magnification is `m = +1`. This is quick recall from earlier classes; the main exam work in this chapter is on curved mirrors and lenses.

## 3. Spherical mirrors: parts and meaning


### 3.1 What is a spherical mirror?

A **spherical mirror** is a mirror whose reflecting surface is a part of a hollow sphere. Think of a shiny ball cut into a small piece.

*Table 3.1 — The two spherical mirrors*

| Concave mirror | Convex mirror |
| --- | --- |
| Reflecting surface is curved **inward**, like the inside of a spoon. | Reflecting surface bulges **outward**, like the back of a spoon. |
| Converges parallel rays after reflection. | Diverges parallel rays after reflection. |
| Focus is **real**: rays really meet in front of the mirror. | Focus is **virtual**: rays appear to come from behind the mirror. |
| Can form real or virtual images depending on object position. | Always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object. |


### 3.2 Terms you must label correctly

- **Pole (P):** the centre point of the mirror surface. All distances are measured from P.
- **Centre of curvature (C):** the centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part. It is not on the mirror.
- **Radius of curvature (R):** distance between P and C.
- **Principal axis:** the straight line joining P and C.
- **Aperture:** the width of the mirror's reflecting surface. Small-aperture diagrams are easier and more accurate.
- **Principal focus (F):** the point where rays parallel and close to the principal axis meet (concave) or appear to come from (convex).
- **Focal length (f):** distance between P and F.
- For a spherical mirror of small aperture: `R = 2f`, so `f = R / 2`.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 3.1 — A concave mirror brings parallel rays to its real focus F. C, F and P lie on the principal axis.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 3.2 — A convex mirror spreads parallel rays. Their backward extensions appear to come from virtual focus F.


### 3.3 Why do the rays converge or diverge?

At every point of a curved mirror, draw a **normal** along the radius to C. The incident ray and reflected ray must make equal angles with that local normal. On a concave mirror the surface is shaped so these reflected rays come together. On a convex mirror they spread apart. This is simply the law of reflection applied many times.


### 3.4 Everyday uses — with the reason

*Table 3.2 — Applications of spherical mirrors*

| Device | Mirror/lens | Why it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Torch, searchlight, car headlight | Concave mirror | A bulb at F gives a strong, nearly parallel beam after reflection. |
| Shaving mirror / makeup mirror | Concave mirror | Face between F and P gives a large, erect, virtual image. |
| Dentist's mirror | Concave mirror | Gives an enlarged erect image of a tooth when held close. |
| Solar furnace / solar cooker | Concave mirror | Concentrates sunlight at the focus and produces heat. |
| Vehicle rear-view mirror | Convex mirror | Always erect and gives a wide field of view because the image is small. |
| Shop/security mirror at corners | Convex mirror | Shows a large area in one small mirror. |

## 4. Ray diagrams and images formed by mirrors


### 4.1 Four useful rays — learn these, not the full tables

To locate an image, draw any **two** of these rays from the top of the object. Where the reflected rays meet, or appear to meet, is the top of the image.

1. **Ray parallel to the principal axis:** after reflection, it passes through F in a concave mirror; in a convex mirror it appears to come from F behind the mirror.
2. **Ray passing through F (concave) or directed towards F (convex):** after reflection, it becomes parallel to the principal axis.
3. **Ray passing through C (concave) or directed towards C (convex):** it falls normally on the mirror and returns along the same path.
4. **Ray striking the pole P:** it reflects obeying `i = r` with the principal axis as the normal.

> **Study tip: Drawing tip** — Use a ruler, put arrows on every ray, and draw **dashed lines** for backward extensions behind a mirror. A virtual image is also usually drawn with dashed lines.


### 4.2 Concave mirror: complete image table

*Table 4.1 — Images formed by a concave mirror*

| Object position | Image position | Nature and size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At infinity | At F | Real, inverted, highly diminished (point-sized). |
| Beyond C | Between F and C | Real, inverted, diminished. |
| At C | At C | Real, inverted, same size. |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | Real, inverted, enlarged. |
| At F | At infinity | Real, inverted, highly enlarged. |
| Between F and P | Behind the mirror | Virtual, erect, enlarged. |

**Memory line:** as the object moves from infinity towards the concave mirror, the real image moves away from F and becomes larger. Once the object crosses F, the image suddenly becomes virtual and erect behind the mirror.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 4.1 — Object beyond C: real, inverted and diminished image between F and C.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 4.2 — Object between C and F: real, inverted and enlarged image beyond C.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 4.3 — Object between F and P: virtual, erect and enlarged image behind the mirror.


### 4.3 Convex mirror: complete image table

*Table 4.2 — Images formed by a convex mirror*

| Object position | Image position | Nature and size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At infinity | At F, behind the mirror | Virtual, erect, highly diminished (point-sized). |
| Between infinity and P | Between P and F, behind the mirror | Virtual, erect, diminished. |

> **[Diagram]** Figure 4.4 — A convex mirror always gives a virtual, erect and diminished image between P and F for a real object.

> **Key point: One-line memory** — **Concave:** can give real/inverted or virtual/erect images. **Convex:** with a real object, always virtual, erect and smaller. This is why convex mirrors are used as rear-view mirrors.

## 5. Mirror formula, sign convention and magnification


### 5.1 New Cartesian sign convention for mirrors

Place the object on the **left** of the mirror so that incident light travels from left to right. Take the pole P as the origin and the principal axis as the x-axis.

- All distances are measured from **P**.
- Distances measured in the **direction of incident light** (to the right) are positive; distances to the left are negative.
- Heights measured **above** the principal axis are positive; heights below it are negative.
- The object is above the axis, so `h` is positive. A real inverted image has `h'` negative; a virtual erect image has `h'` positive.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 5.1 — The same sign rule is used for mirrors and lenses: right/above are positive, left/below are negative.

*Table 5.1 — Signs for a mirror with the object on the left*

| Quantity | Common sign | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object distance `u` | Negative | Object is on the left of P. |
| Focal length `f` of concave mirror | Negative | Its focus is in front of the mirror, on the left. |
| Focal length `f` of convex mirror | Positive | Its focus is behind the mirror, on the right. |
| Image distance `v`, real image | Negative | Real image is in front, on the left. |
| Image distance `v`, virtual image | Positive | Virtual image is behind, on the right. |
| Image height `h'`, inverted | Negative | Image lies below the principal axis. |
| Image height `h'`, erect | Positive | Image lies above the principal axis. |


### 5.2 Mirror formula and magnification

> **Formula:** `1/v + 1/u = 1/f`
> Mirror formula — use signs for u, v and f

> **Formula:** `m = h'/h = -v/u`
> Linear magnification produced by a spherical mirror

- `u` = object distance from P; `v` = image distance from P; `f` = focal length; `h` = object height; `h'` = image height.
- Use **one unit throughout** a problem: all cm or all m. Do not mix cm and m.
- `m` has no unit because it is a ratio of two lengths.
- `|m| > 1` means enlarged; `|m| < 1` means diminished; `|m| = 1` means same size.
- For a real object in this chapter, `m < 0` means real and inverted; `m > 0` means virtual and erect.
- **Positive `v` for a mirror does not mean a real image.** For a mirror, a real image is on the same side as the object and has `v < 0`.


### 5.3 Worked mirror numericals


#### Example 5.1 — Concave mirror, real image

A 4 cm tall object is placed 20 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image position, size and nature.

1. Given: `u = -20 cm`, `f = -15 cm`, `h = +4 cm`.
2. `1/v + 1/u = 1/f`, so `1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/15 + 1/20 = -1/60`.
3. Therefore `v = -60 cm`. The negative sign shows a real image 60 cm in front of the mirror.
4. `m = -v/u = -(-60)/(-20) = -3` and `h' = m × h = -3 × 4 = -12 cm`.
5. **Answer:** real, inverted, 12 cm tall, 60 cm in front of the mirror.


#### Example 5.2 — Convex mirror, virtual image

An object is 20 cm in front of a convex mirror whose radius of curvature is 30 cm. Find the image position and magnification.

1. `R = +30 cm`, so `f = R/2 = +15 cm`; `u = -20 cm`.
2. `1/v = 1/f - 1/u = 1/15 + 1/20 = 7/60`, so `v = 60/7 ≈ +8.6 cm`.
3. `m = -v/u = -(60/7)/(-20) = 3/7 ≈ +0.43`.
4. **Answer:** virtual and erect image about 8.6 cm behind the mirror, about 0.43 times the object size.


#### Example 5.3 — Object at C

A concave mirror has `f = -20 cm` and an object is placed at `u = -40 cm`. Find `v` and `m`.

1. `1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/20 + 1/40 = -1/40`, so `v = -40 cm`.
2. `m = -v/u = -(-40)/(-40) = -1`.
3. **Answer:** image is at C, real, inverted and the same size. This matches the ray table.

> **Warning: Most common mirror mistakes** — Using `+f` for a concave mirror, using the lens formula by accident, writing `m = v/u` instead of `-v/u`, or forgetting to convert `R` into `f` with `f = R/2`.

## 6. Refraction of light


### 6.1 What is refraction?

When light travels **obliquely** from one transparent medium to another, its speed changes and its path usually bends. This change in direction is called **refraction**.

**Why does it bend?** Light has different speeds in different materials. One side of a wavefront slows down or speeds up before the other side, so the whole ray turns. The colour/frequency stays the same; speed and wavelength change.

*Table 6.1 — Three cases to remember*

| Change of medium | Speed | Direction of bending | Angle relationship |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rarer → denser (air → glass/water) | Decreases | Towards the normal | `r < i` |
| Denser → rarer (glass/water → air) | Increases | Away from the normal | `r > i` |
| Along the normal (`i = 0°`) | Changes | No change in direction | `r = 0°` |

> **Key point: Optical density is not mass density** — A medium with a larger refractive index is called **optically denser**. This does not mean it is physically heavier. For example, kerosene floats on water but is optically denser than water.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 6.1 — Air to glass: light slows down and bends towards the normal. The dashed line shows the original straight path.


### 6.2 Laws of refraction

1. The **incident ray, refracted ray and normal** at the point of incidence all lie in the **same plane**.
2. For a given pair of media and a given colour of light, the ratio `sin i / sin r` is constant. This is **Snell's law**.

> **Formula:** `n1 sin i = n2 sin r`
> General form of Snell's law

> **Formula:** `n21 = sin i / sin r = n2/n1 = v1/v2`
> Relative refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1

Here `n1` and `n2` are absolute refractive indices, and `v1` and `v2` are the speeds of light in media 1 and 2. Also, `n12 = 1/n21`.


### 6.3 Absolute refractive index

The **absolute refractive index** of a medium tells us how much slower light travels in that medium than in vacuum (or, approximately, air).

> **Formula:** `n = c / v`
> Absolute refractive index; c = speed of light in vacuum, v = speed in the medium

- `n` has **no unit** because it is a ratio of two speeds.
- Vacuum has `n = 1`; air is about `1.0003` and is usually taken as 1.
- A larger `n` means a lower speed of light and a stronger bending effect.
- For ordinary transparent media, absolute `n` is greater than 1. A **relative** refractive index can be less than 1.

*Table 6.2 — Common values (small variations are normal)*

| Medium | Approximate refractive index n | Easy comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vacuum | 1.00 | Light has its maximum speed. |
| Air | 1.0003 ≈ 1.00 | Almost the same as vacuum. |
| Water | 1.33 | Light travels slower than in air. |
| Crown glass | 1.52 | Bends light more than water does. |
| Diamond | 2.42 | Light slows a lot; strong optical effects. |


### 6.4 Refraction through a rectangular glass slab

A rectangular glass slab has two **parallel** faces. Light refracts once when it enters the slab and again when it leaves. The second bending cancels the change in direction from the first bending.

- At air → glass, the ray bends **towards** the normal.
- At glass → air, it bends **away** from the normal.
- The **emergent ray is parallel** to the incident ray: `i = e`.
- The emergent ray is shifted sideways. This perpendicular shift is called **lateral displacement**.
- A thicker slab, a larger angle of incidence or a larger refractive index generally gives a larger shift.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 6.2 — Refraction through a rectangular glass slab: the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced.


### 6.5 Worked refraction numericals


#### Example 6.1 — Speed of light in glass

1. Given `n_glass = 1.50` and `c = 3 × 10^8 m/s`.
2. `n = c/v`, so `v = c/n = (3 × 10^8)/1.50 = 2 × 10^8 m/s`.
3. **Answer:** light travels at `2 × 10^8 m/s` in the glass.


#### Example 6.2 — Finding refractive index from angles

Light goes from air into a transparent material. The angle of incidence is `30°` and the angle of refraction is `19°`. Estimate the refractive index of the material.

1. `sin 30° = 0.500` and `sin 19° ≈ 0.326`.
2. `n ≈ sin i / sin r = 0.500/0.326 ≈ 1.53`.
3. **Answer:** about `1.53`, close to crown glass.


#### Example 6.3 — Relative refractive index

1. Given `n_glass = 1.52` and `n_water = 1.33`.
2. Glass with respect to water: `n_gw = n_glass/n_water = 1.52/1.33 ≈ 1.14`.
3. Water with respect to glass: `n_wg = 1/n_gw ≈ 0.88`.

## 7. Spherical lenses: parts and ray rules


### 7.1 Convex and concave lenses

A **lens** is a piece of transparent material bounded by two spherical surfaces. It works by refraction, not reflection.

*Table 7.1 — The two spherical lenses*

| Convex lens | Concave lens |
| --- | --- |
| Thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges. | Thinner in the middle and thicker at the edges. |
| **Converging lens**: brings parallel rays together. | **Diverging lens**: spreads parallel rays apart. |
| Has a real principal focus on the other side of the lens. | Has a virtual principal focus on the object side. |
| Can form real/inverted or virtual/erect images. | Always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object. |
| `f` is positive; power is positive. | `f` is negative; power is negative. |


### 7.2 Points and distances on a lens diagram

- **Optical centre (O):** the central point of a thin lens. A ray passing through O goes nearly undeviated.
- **Principal axis:** the straight line passing through O and the two centres of curvature.
- **Aperture:** the effective diameter of the lens.
- **First focus F1:** focus on the object side. **Second focus F2:** focus on the other side.
- **Focal length f:** distance from O to F1 or F2. For a thin lens in air, both sides have the same magnitude of f.
- **2F1 and 2F2:** points at a distance `2f` from O on the two sides. They are very useful in ray diagrams.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 7.1 — Convex and concave lenses with O, F1, F2, 2F1 and 2F2 marked on the principal axis.


### 7.3 Three ray rules for lenses

1. A ray **parallel to the principal axis** passes through `F2` after a convex lens; after a concave lens it appears to come from `F1`.
2. A ray passing through `F1` before a convex lens emerges **parallel** to the axis. A ray directed towards `F2` before a concave lens emerges parallel.
3. A ray passing through the **optical centre O** continues almost without deviation for a thin lens.

> **[Diagram]** Figure 7.2 — Three standard rays used to construct lens ray diagrams. Dashed lines are backward extensions.


### 7.4 Everyday uses

*Table 7.2 — Applications of spherical lenses*

| Use | Lens | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnifying glass | Convex | Object inside f gives a virtual, erect and enlarged image. |
| Camera and projector | Convex | Can form a real image on film/sensor or a screen. |
| Simple microscope/telescope | Convex | Combines enlarged real/virtual images. |
| Spectacle lens for far-sightedness | Convex | Converges incoming rays; details are in the next chapter. |
| Spectacle lens for near-sightedness | Concave | Diverges incoming rays; details are in the next chapter. |
| Door peephole / some wide-view systems | Concave | Gives a small erect image of a wider area. |

## 9. Lens formula, magnification and power


### 9.1 Sign convention for lenses

Keep the object on the **left** and let incident light travel left to right. Take O as the origin. Distances to the right are positive and distances to the left are negative; heights above the axis are positive and below are negative.

*Table 9.1 — Signs for a lens with the object on the left*

| Quantity | Common sign | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Object distance `u` | Negative | Object is on the left of O. |
| Focal length `f` of convex lens | Positive | Second focus F2 is on the right. |
| Focal length `f` of concave lens | Negative | Its focus is virtual, on the left. |
| Image distance `v`, real image | Positive | Real image is formed on the right. |
| Image distance `v`, virtual image | Negative | Virtual image is on the left. |


### 9.2 Lens formula and magnification

> **Formula:** `1/v - 1/u = 1/f`
> Lens formula — notice the minus sign

> **Formula:** `m = h'/h = v/u`
> Linear magnification produced by a spherical lens

- Use one length unit throughout: all cm or all m.
- `m < 0` means inverted and real; `m > 0` means erect and virtual (for a real object).
- `|m| > 1`, `|m| < 1` and `|m| = 1` mean enlarged, diminished and same size respectively.
- The mirror magnification has `-v/u`; the lens magnification has `v/u`. Do not interchange them.


### 9.3 Power of a lens

The **power** of a lens tells us how strongly it converges or diverges light. A lens with a short focal length bends light more and has greater power.

> **Formula:** `P = 1 / f (in metres)`
> Power in dioptres

> **Formula:** `P = 100 / f (in cm)     and     P_total = P1 + P2 + ...`
> Quick conversion and thin lenses in contact

- The SI unit of power is the **dioptre (D)**, where `1 D = 1 m^-1`.
- A convex lens has **positive** power; a concave lens has **negative** power.
- **One dioptre** is the power of a lens of focal length `1 m`.
- For thin lenses kept in contact, simply add their powers with signs.
- If focal length doubles, power becomes half; if focal length halves, power doubles.

*Table 9.2 — Power calculations*

| Focal length | Power | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `+0.50 m` or `+50 cm` | `+2 D` | Convex/converging |
| `+0.25 m` or `+25 cm` | `+4 D` | Convex/converging |
| `-0.40 m` or `-40 cm` | `-2.5 D` | Concave/diverging |
| `-2.00 m` or `-200 cm` | `-0.5 D` | Concave/diverging |


### 9.4 Worked lens numericals


#### Example 9.1 — Convex lens, real image

A 5 cm object is 25 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the image.

1. Given `u = -25 cm`, `f = +10 cm`, `h = +5 cm`.
2. `1/v - 1/u = 1/f`, so `1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/10 - 1/25 = 3/50`.
3. `v = +50/3 ≈ +16.7 cm`: a real image on the other side.
4. `m = v/u = (50/3)/(-25) = -2/3`; `h' = m × h ≈ -3.33 cm`.
5. **Answer:** real, inverted, about 3.33 cm tall, 16.7 cm from the lens.


#### Example 9.2 — Concave lens, virtual image

A 6 cm object is 30 cm from a concave lens of focal length 20 cm. Find the image.

1. Given `u = -30 cm`, `f = -20 cm`, `h = +6 cm`.
2. `1/v = 1/f + 1/u = -1/20 - 1/30 = -1/12`, so `v = -12 cm`.
3. `m = v/u = (-12)/(-30) = +0.4`; `h' = +2.4 cm`.
4. **Answer:** virtual, erect, 2.4 cm tall, 12 cm on the object side.


#### Example 9.3 — Lens power and focal length

1. A lens has `P = +4 D`. Then `f = 1/P = +0.25 m = +25 cm`; it is convex.
2. A lens has `f = -50 cm = -0.50 m`. Then `P = 1/f = -2 D`; it is concave.


#### Example 9.4 — Two lenses in contact

Thin lenses of powers `+2.5 D` and `-1.5 D` are placed in contact. Find the total power and focal length.

1. `P_total = +2.5 + (-1.5) = +1.0 D`.
2. `f_total = 1/P_total = +1.0 m = +100 cm`.
3. **Answer:** the combination behaves like a converging convex lens of focal length 1 m.


### 9.5 Mirror or lens? Keep the formulas separate

*Table 9.3 — Formula comparison*

| Feature | Spherical mirror | Spherical lens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main process | Reflection | Refraction |
| Formula | `1/v + 1/u = 1/f` | `1/v - 1/u = 1/f` |
| Magnification | `m = -v/u` | `m = v/u` |
| Concave device | Concave mirror: `f < 0` | Concave lens: `f < 0` |
| Convex device | Convex mirror: `f > 0` | Convex lens: `f > 0` |
| Power | Not asked in this chapter | `P = 1/f (m)` |

## 10. Practical work and lab skills


### 10.1 Finding the focal length of a concave mirror

A very distant object sends almost parallel rays. A concave mirror brings those rays to its principal focus, so the distance from P to the sharp image gives approximately `f`.

1. Fix the concave mirror on a stand and point it at a distant tree or building. **Never use the Sun directly.**
2. Place a white screen in front of the reflecting surface, on the same side as the distant object.
3. Move the screen until a small, sharp, inverted image is formed.
4. Measure the distance from the pole P of the mirror to the screen. Repeat and take the mean.
5. **Result:** mean distance ≈ focal length `f` of the concave mirror.


### 10.2 Finding the focal length of a convex lens

1. Hold the convex lens facing a distant tree or building.
2. Put a white screen on the other side of the lens and move it until a sharp, small, inverted image appears.
3. Measure the distance from the optical centre O to the screen. Repeat and take the mean.
4. **Result:** mean distance ≈ focal length `f` of the convex lens.


### 10.3 Tracing a ray through a rectangular glass slab

1. Place the glass slab on white paper and draw its boundary.
2. Fix two pins in a straight line to represent the incident ray striking one face obliquely.
3. Looking from the opposite face, fix two more pins so that all four pins appear in one straight line.
4. Remove the slab and pins; join the pin positions to draw the incident and emergent rays.
5. Join the points of incidence and emergence to draw the refracted ray inside the slab. Draw normals and measure `i`, `r` and `e` with a protractor.
6. Repeat for another angle of incidence.

- **Observation:** `i` is approximately equal to `e`; the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.
- **Result:** the ray is laterally displaced because it bends towards the normal on entry and away from it on exit.
- **Precautions:** use a sharp pencil, keep pins vertical and well separated, use an oblique angle, and do not shift the slab while tracing.

> **Study tip: Practical viva tip** — If asked why a distant object is used, say: **rays from a distant object are nearly parallel, so they meet at the principal focus.** This one sentence answers both focal-length experiments.

## 11. Formula sheet and rapid revision


### 11.1 Every formula in one place

*Table 11.1 — One-page formula sheet*

| Formula | Meaning | Important sign/unit point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `R = 2f` or `f = R/2` | Radius and focal length of a spherical mirror | Use magnitude; apply sign separately. |
| `1/v + 1/u = 1/f` | Mirror formula | The sign between terms is **plus**. |
| `m = h'/h = -v/u` | Mirror magnification | The minus sign is part of the formula. |
| `n = c/v` | Absolute refractive index | No unit; ordinary transparent media have `n > 1`. |
| `n1 sin i = n2 sin r` | Snell's law | Angles are measured from the normal. |
| `n21 = n2/n1 = v1/v2 = sin i/sin r` | Relative refractive index | `n12 = 1/n21`. |
| `i = e` | Rectangular glass slab | Incident and emergent rays are parallel. |
| `1/v - 1/u = 1/f` | Lens formula | The sign between terms is **minus**. |
| `m = h'/h = v/u` | Lens magnification | There is **no minus sign** here. |
| `P = 1/f (m)` | Power of a lens | `f` must be in metres; unit is dioptre (D). |
| `P = 100/f (cm)` | Power when `f` is in centimetres | Keep the sign of `f`. |
| `P_total = P1 + P2 + ...` | Thin lenses in contact | Add powers with signs. |


### 11.2 Last-day revision in simple lines


#### Mirrors

- Concave converges; convex diverges.
- Concave mirror can make real/inverted or virtual/erect images; convex mirror always gives virtual, erect, smaller images for a real object.
- Object at C gives image at C, same size and inverted.
- Object between F and P of a concave mirror gives a large erect virtual image.
- Mirror formula is `1/v + 1/u = 1/f`; mirror magnification is `-v/u`.


#### Refraction

- Air → glass/water: slower, towards normal. Glass/water → air: faster, away from normal.
- Normal incidence: speed changes but direction does not.
- `n = c/v`; larger `n` means lower speed.
- In a rectangular glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray and laterally shifted.


#### Lenses

- Convex converges and has `f > 0`, `P > 0`; concave diverges and has `f < 0`, `P < 0`.
- Convex lens can make real/inverted or virtual/erect images; concave lens always gives virtual, erect, smaller images for a real object.
- Object at 2F1 of a convex lens gives image at 2F2, same size and inverted.
- Lens formula is `1/v - 1/u = 1/f`; lens magnification is `v/u`.
- `P = 1/f (m)`; add powers for thin lenses in contact.


### 11.3 Tiny glossary

*Table 11.2 — Words examiners expect you to know*

| Word | Easy meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Normal | Line at 90° to a surface at the point where light strikes. |
| Pole P | Middle point of a mirror surface. |
| Optical centre O | Middle point of a thin lens; a ray through it goes nearly straight. |
| Focus F | Point where parallel rays meet or appear to come from. |
| Principal axis | Main straight line through P/O and the focus. |
| Real image | An image that can be caught on a screen. |
| Virtual image | An image that cannot be caught on a screen. |
| Lateral displacement | Sideways shift of the emergent ray from a glass slab. |
| Dioptre | Unit of lens power; `1 D = 1 m^-1`. |
| Aperture | Useful width/diameter of a mirror or lens. |

> **Key point: The one sign check that saves most marks** — Object is on the left, so `u` is negative. Concave mirror and concave lens have negative `f`; convex mirror and convex lens have positive `f`. Then choose the correct mirror/lens formula.

## 12. How to solve numericals without fear


### 12.1 A five-step method

1. **Write the given values with signs and units.** Example: `u = -25 cm`, `f = -15 cm`.
2. **Choose the device:** mirror or lens. Write the correct formula before substituting.
3. **Substitute carefully.** Keep all lengths in cm or all in metres.
4. **Solve for the unknown.** Keep fractions until the last step to avoid rounding errors.
5. **Interpret the answer:** positive/negative `v`, `m` and `h'` tell you where the image is and what it looks like.


### 12.2 Worked mixed examples


#### Example 12.1 — Object inside the focus of a concave mirror

A concave mirror has radius 30 cm. An object is 10 cm in front of it. Find `v` and `m`.

1. `R = 30 cm`, so `f = -15 cm`; `u = -10 cm`.
2. `1/v = 1/f - 1/u = -1/15 + 1/10 = 1/30`, so `v = +30 cm`.
3. `m = -v/u = -30/(-10) = +3`.
4. **Answer:** virtual, erect and three times enlarged image 30 cm behind the mirror.


#### Example 12.2 — Convex lens with a distant object

A convex lens has `f = +20 cm`. An object is 60 cm away. Find the image position and magnification.

1. `1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/20 - 1/60 = 1/30`, so `v = +30 cm`.
2. `m = v/u = 30/(-60) = -0.5`.
3. **Answer:** real, inverted, half-sized image 30 cm on the other side of the lens.


#### Example 12.3 — Start from lens power

A lens has power `-2.5 D`. An 8 cm object is 60 cm from it. Find the image height.

1. `f = 1/P = -0.40 m = -40 cm`; the lens is concave.
2. `1/v = 1/f + 1/u = -1/40 - 1/60 = -1/24`, so `v = -24 cm`.
3. `m = v/u = (-24)/(-60) = +0.4`; `h' = 0.4 × 8 = +3.2 cm`.
4. **Answer:** virtual, erect, 3.2 cm tall.


#### Example 12.4 — Refractive index and speed

Light travels in a medium at `1.875 × 10^8 m/s`. Find its refractive index.

1. `n = c/v = (3 × 10^8)/(1.875 × 10^8) = 1.6`.
2. **Answer:** `n = 1.6`; light travels 1.6 times slower than in vacuum.

> **Warning: Five fast error checks** — Did I use the **mirror** formula on a mirror question? Did I convert `R` to `f`? Did I put a **negative sign** on `u`? Did I use **metres** for power? Did I state the image nature at the end?

Choose **(A)** if both statements are true and the Reason correctly explains the Assertion; **(B)** if both are true but the Reason is not the correct explanation; **(C)** if Assertion is true but Reason is false; **(D)** if Assertion is false but Reason is true.

## 14. Answer key and hints


### 14.1 MCQ answers

*Table 14.1 — MCQ key*

| Q | Answer | One-line reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | b | A concave mirror can form a real enlarged image when the object is between C and F. |
| 2 | b | Along the normal, the angle with the normal is 0°. |
| 3 | a | A convex mirror has a virtual focus behind it, so `f > 0`. |
| 4 | b | Lens power is measured in dioptres (D). |
| 5 | a | Air to glass is rarer to denser; speed decreases and the ray bends towards the normal. |
| 6 | b | The lens formula is `1/v - 1/u = 1/f`. |
| 7 | c | A concave lens always gives virtual, erect and diminished images for a real object. |
| 8 | b | Negative `m` means inverted; `\|m\| = 2` means enlarged. |
| 9 | b | `n = c/v`; larger `n` means smaller `v`. |
| 10 | b | `f = 1/P = 1/4 = 0.25 m`, positive for a convex lens. |
| 11 | b | Two parallel faces make the emergent ray parallel but laterally shifted. |
| 12 | c | `+2 + (+1) = +3 D`, a positive net power. |


### 14.2 Assertion–Reason answers

1. **A** — a convex mirror gives an erect small image and covers a wide field.
2. **A** — the decrease in speed causes bending towards the normal.
3. **A** — `P = 1/f` and a concave lens has `f < 0`.
4. **D** — the Assertion is false (for a real inverted image, `m < 0`); the Reason is true.
5. **A** — equal and opposite changes at the two parallel faces make `i = e`.
6. **B** — both statements are true, but the optical-centre ray rule does not explain diamond's refractive index.


### 14.3 Short-answer key points

1. Same plane; `i = r`; angles are measured from the normal.
2. Always erect image; small image gives a wider field of view.
3. F is where parallel rays meet; `f = PF`; `R = 2f`.
4. `sin i/sin r` is constant; more generally `n1 sin i = n2 sin r`; `n = c/v`.
5. The two faces are parallel, so the bending on entry is reversed on exit; `i = e`.
6. One dioptre is `1 m^-1`; `P = 1/f` and `f < 0` for a concave lens, so `P < 0`.
7. Real: rays actually meet, can be caught on a screen, usually inverted. Virtual: rays appear to meet, cannot be caught, usually erect.
8. A source at F gives a parallel reflected beam, concentrating light in front of the vehicle.
9. No. At normal incidence, speed and wavelength change but the direction does not change, so there is no visible bending.


### 14.4 Numerical answers with working


#### Question 1

`u = -25 cm`, `f = -15 cm`. `1/v = -1/15 + 1/25 = -2/75`, so `v = -37.5 cm`. `m = -v/u = -1.5` and `h' = -6 cm`. **Real, inverted, 6 cm tall image 37.5 cm in front.**


#### Question 2

`f = +20 cm`, `u = -30 cm`. `1/v = 1/20 + 1/30 = 1/12`, so `v = +12 cm`; `m = -12/(-30) = +0.4`. **Virtual, erect image 12 cm behind the mirror.**


#### Question 3

`v = c/n = (3 × 10^8)/1.50 = 2 × 10^8 m/s`.


#### Question 4

`n ≈ sin 60°/sin 35° = 0.866/0.574 ≈ 1.51`.


#### Question 5

`u = -20 cm`, `f = +15 cm`. `1/v = 1/15 - 1/20 = 1/60`, so `v = +60 cm`. `m = 60/(-20) = -3`; `h' = -9 cm`. **Real, inverted, 9 cm tall image.**


#### Question 6

`u = -45 cm`, `f = -15 cm`. `1/v = -1/15 - 1/45 = -4/45`, so `v = -11.25 cm`; `m = (-11.25)/(-45) = +0.25`. **Virtual, erect, quarter-sized image.**


#### Question 7

`P_total = +5 + (-2) = +3 D`; `f = 1/3 m ≈ +0.333 m = +33.3 cm`. **Converging combination.**


#### Question 8

`f = -80 cm = -0.80 m`; `P = 1/f = -1.25 D`. **Concave/diverging lens.**

## 15. Common mistakes and final checklist


### 15.1 Mistake → correction

*Table 15.1 — Fix these before the exam*

| Common mistake | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Measuring `i` and `r` from the mirror surface. | Always measure from the **normal**. |
| Using `1/v - 1/u = 1/f` for a mirror. | Mirror formula has a **plus**: `1/v + 1/u = 1/f`. |
| Using `-v/u` for lens magnification. | Lens magnification is `v/u`; mirror magnification is `-v/u`. |
| Taking `f = R` instead of `R/2`. | For a spherical mirror, `f = R/2`. |
| Giving `f` in cm inside `P = 1/f`. | Convert to **metres** first, or use `P = 100/f(cm)`. |
| Calling the image real just because `v` is positive. | For a lens, `v > 0` usually means real; for a mirror, a real image has `v < 0`. |
| Saying a convex mirror gives an enlarged image. | It gives a **small**, virtual, erect image for a real object. |
| Saying a concave lens can form a real image of a real object at Class 10 level. | It always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image. |
| Forgetting that normal incidence still changes speed. | There is no visible bending, but the speed and wavelength still change. |
| Drawing backward extensions as solid rays. | Use **dashed lines** for virtual extensions and virtual images. |
| Mixing cm and m in one calculation. | Choose one unit and write it beside every value. |
